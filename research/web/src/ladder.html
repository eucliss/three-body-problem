The Three-BodyLadder

Two equal stars and a lighter third can repeat the same dance forever if they start in exactly the right way. We searched masses below 0.5, which the published planar catalogues of this problem do not cover, and found a family of these repeating orbits built like a ladder: on rung k the two heavy stars wind around each other k times, then unwind. Rungs 1 and 2 were already known. Rungs 3 to 11 are new.

iThe family

Each rung adds one turn

All five orbits below start from the same kind of position: the two heavy stars at ±1 on a line, the light star exactly between them, everything moving with zero total momentum and zero total spin. Only the launch velocity differs.

The motion is the same story on every rung of the main family. The light star swings out on a long loop to one side while the heavy pair winds around each other k times. Exactly half a period later the light star falls back through the middle, the three stars line up as they started (mirrored), and the light star swings out to the other side while the pair unwinds the same k turns. Because the total spin is zero, the pair's winding is always balanced by the light star's swing. Each rung up the ladder adds one more turn in each direction. (The count holds to within 0.03 turns for m₃ ≤ 0.5; near equal masses rung 1 overshoots to about 1.3 turns. The second rung-3 orbit, 3′, winds only twice.)

The rungs are spaced almost exactly evenly in time. At m₃ = 0.25 each extra turn adds 8.96 to 9.02 time units to the period, from k = 3 all the way to k = 11. This is an instance of what Dmitrašinović and Šuvakov (2015) called the topological dependence of Kepler's third law: the scale-free period grows in step with the length of the orbit's word. We used that spacing to predict where the next rung should be before we found it. The launch velocities crowd together as k grows, each rung starting a little closer to the one before, as though the ladder were climbing toward a limit.

iiTopology

How an orbit gets its name

Two orbits can look different on screen and still be the same orbit seen from a different scale or angle. To tell orbits apart, the field uses a fingerprint that ignores size and rotation: Montgomery's shape sphere. Every triangle the three stars can form is one point on this sphere. Equilateral triangles sit at the poles. Straight-line arrangements, where one star sits between the other two, lie on the equator. The three collision points are holes in the equator that no orbit can cross.

Each time the stars line up, the curve crosses the equator. Reading the crossings in pairs and noting which arc each one used turns the path into a word in four letters. The word records how the orbit winds around the collision holes, so any orbit that can be smoothly deformed into another without passing through a collision gets the same word. That makes it the right key for comparing against catalogues.

The rung-k word has 4k + 4 letters; the two forms are the same word read from different starting letters. We use the conventions of Šuvakov & Dmitrašinović (2013) and Li & Liao (2017), and confirmed them by reproducing the published words of 12 known orbits exactly. With k = 0 the word would be that of the famous figure-eight orbit.

iiiDiscovery

Where the ladder lives

Published surveys of this family of starting conditions looked at a handful of masses for the third star: 0.5, 0.75, 1, 2, 4, 5, 8 and 10. We searched below 0.5, where the planar catalogues we could check list no orbits. (Li & Liao's three-dimensional survey covers m₃ = 0.1 to 2 in a wider set of starting conditions; none of its near-planar orbits matches ours. We could not run a wider literature search.) Then we followed rungs 1 to 6 continuously as the third star gets heavier; rungs 7 to 11 would not continue away from m₃ = 0.25 in our runs. Each curve is one rung, traced through 90 to 1,000 computed orbits. Our continuation of the higher rungs stops at lighter third stars (k = 2 at 0.85, 3 at 0.76, 4 at 0.71, 5 at 0.63, 6 at 0.56). Whether the families end there is open.

ivMethod

How we found it

For each mass we launched about half a million trial orbits on a fine grid of starting velocities and watched each one for up to 100 time units. A periodic orbit returns exactly to where it began, so we kept every trial that came back close to its own start. The map shows that closeness for every trial (400 × 400 here, watched to t = 60). Bright threads are near-returns. The few points where a thread pinches to a single bright dot are the periodic orbits. The new rungs sit in one tight cluster along the edge of a bright thread: each rung starts almost where the last one did. The large glowing region at the bottom of the m₃ = 0.5 map surrounds a short catalogued orbit (T = 4.18, with the figure-eight's word), and orbits started there stay near their start for a long time; we have not classified the motion there. The map watches each trial only to t = 60, so rungs with longer periods cannot show up as dots on it.

  5. Compare. Check the word against all 2,044 published words for this kind of orbit, and the numbers against all published starting conditions.

As a control we ran the same scan at m₃ = 0.5, a mass the 2018 catalogue covers. It refined 8 distinct orbits. Seven are catalogue orbits. The eighth (v₁ = 0.2550328, v₂ = 0.2707765, T = 28.24322) has the word of the catalogued I.A i.c. 4 but a different scale-free period (20.52 against 19.09), so it is a different orbit that the catalogue does not list. Our pipeline marks orbits as known by word, so it filed this one as known; we have not reviewed it further. The same scan also missed the ladder at m₃ = 0.5, including the catalogued rungs 1 and 2; the rungs shown at that mass come from following the family up from m₃ = 0.25. A grid scan misses orbits, and that is presumably how the 2018 survey missed rungs 3 to 5.

vVerification

How we checked it

A claim of a new orbit can fail in three ways: the orbit is not really periodic, it is really a known orbit under another name, or it was in the literature all along. Independent reviewers with their own code re-integrated every orbit and recomputed the words of the first 16, and compared every word with all 2,360 Li & Liao catalogue words under every physical symmetry. The third check, a search of the wider literature, could not be run here. So new means absent from the catalogues we could check.

All rungs are unstable

None of these orbits would survive in a real star system. A tiny nudge grows by the factor shown below each time the orbit repeats. At m₃ = 0.25 that factor climbs from ×42 at rung 3 to ×850 at rung 11, but the growth per unit time falls, because each period is longer. They are the hidden skeleton of the chaotic motion around them: the unstable repeating paths that chaotic orbits shadow for a while before wandering off.

viData

Starting conditions

Every orbit here starts with star 1 at (−1, 0), star 2 at (1, 0) and star 3 at the origin. Stars 1 and 2 both move with velocity (v₁, v₂), and star 3 moves with −2(v₁, v₂)/m₃, so the total momentum and angular momentum are zero. With G = 1, these numbers reproduce each orbit in any good integrator. Check re-integrates the orbit in your browser and reports how closely it returns to its start.

viiScope

What this does and does not show

  • New here means absent from the catalogues of Šuvakov & Dmitrašinović (2013), Li & Liao (2017; 695 equal-mass families), Li, Jing & Liao (2018; 1,349 unequal-mass orbits) and Li & Liao (2019; 316 free-fall orbits), checked by word under every physical symmetry and by starting values. No wider literature search was possible here, and we could not open the Sofia group's equal-mass databases (Hristov, Hristova and coauthors, 2022–2026). If the higher rungs continue all the way to equal masses, some of these words may already be listed there.
  • What we counted. The 23 new orbits are those whose words are new (rungs 3 to 11). Rungs 1 and 2 at m₃ = 0.1 to 0.3, and rung 2 at m₃ = 0.75, are not catalogued at those masses but carry known words, so we do not count them. The grid scans at m₃ = 0.2 and 0.25 also found two orbits whose word is the square of rung 1's; we did not send them for review and do not count them either.
  • The orbits are numerical. Each closes to between 10−11 and 8×10−9 in 64-bit arithmetic, confirmed by an independent integrator. That is strong evidence, but not a computer-assisted proof.
  • The curve ends are not family ends. Our continuation stops where the Newton step no longer converges to our acceptance threshold. We have not determined why; the orbits there are less unstable than others we refined. The branches may continue further.
  • Only one plane of starting conditions. We searched the standard isosceles slice with zero angular momentum and two equal masses. Orbits with spin, three different masses, or motion in three dimensions are outside this search.
  • Nothing here is stable. These orbits are mathematical objects that organize chaotic motion. They are not predictions of systems we would find in the sky.
