The equation

Every body pulls on every other body

Give each body a position {{IEQ:ri}} and a mass {{IEQ:mi}}. Newton's law of gravitation and his second law combine into one equation per body:

{{EQ:main}}

G is the gravitational constant. ε is an optional softening length; real point masses have ε = 0, which is the default here.

That line is fully general. Stars, planets, moons and black holes (far from the horizon) all obey it. The only inputs are three masses and the starting positions and velocities. Written out for three bodies it is:

{{EQ:expanded}}

That makes 18 coupled first-order differential equations: three position components and three velocity components for each body. The same system in Hamiltonian form, which is what the solver actually works with:

{{EQ:hamiltonian}}

The triangle form

Lagrange noticed you can drop the centre of mass entirely and follow the triangle's sides instead. With {{IEQ:sides}}, the sides always close up, and each obeys a Kepler law plus one shared coupling term:

{{EQ:symmetric}}

Without the coupling term S each side would be an independent two-body problem with a closed-form ellipse. That one shared term is where all the difficulty lives.

Planets or stars: one equation at any scale

Conservation

Ten numbers never change

{{EQ:integrals}}

Energy (one number), momentum (three), angular momentum (three) and the steady drift of the centre of mass (three) are fixed by the starting conditions. They shrink the 18-dimensional space of possible states to 8 dimensions.

For two bodies that is enough to solve the motion exactly: Kepler's ellipses. For three it is not, and it never will be. Bruns (1887) proved there are no other conserved quantities that are algebraic in positions and velocities. Poincaré (1890) proved there are no further analytic ones. The motion is not confined to any simpler surface that a formula could describe.

The instrument rail above tracks two of these live. With the default tolerance the energy should stay within about 10⁻¹¹ of its starting value, even through close encounters.

Solving it

What "solving the three-body problem" can mean

There is no general closed-form solution. That is a theorem, not a gap in current knowledge. Three kinds of answer exist instead.

1. A series that converges and is useless

Karl Sundman (1912) proved that the positions can be written as power series that converge for all time whenever the angular momentum is not zero. He did it by regularizing collisions and mapping all of time into a disk:

{{EQ:sundman}}

It is a true solution in the mathematical sense. In practice the series converges so slowly that Beloriszky (1930) estimated about 108,000,000 terms would be needed for ordinary astronomical accuracy.

2. Exact special solutions

Euler (1767) and Lagrange (1772) found families where the bodies keep the same shape forever. Both work for any three masses, and the simulator rebuilds them for whatever masses you set. Lagrange's equilateral triangle rotates at

{{EQ:lagomega}}

It is stable only when one mass dominates, by Routh's criterion (1875):

{{EQ:routh}}

Equal masses fail it, so the equal-mass triangle eventually breaks apart. Rounding errors of 10⁻¹⁶ grow exponentially until they matter. Euler's collinear solution places the bodies on a rotating line, with spacing ratio λ fixed by the single positive root of Euler's quintic:

{{EQ:quintic}}

Since 2013 thousands of further periodic orbits have been found by computer search. The figure-eight, butterflies, moths and yin-yangs in the System menu are among them. Each one here was refined with Newton's method, which tightens the published starting values from about 10⁻³ closure to about 10⁻¹².

3. Numerical integration with controlled error

This is how the problem is solved in practice, and what this page does. For any starting conditions and any finite span of time, the solver produces the motion to a chosen accuracy. The limit is chaos. A tiny difference δ₀ grows exponentially:

{{EQ:lyap}}

Double-precision numbers start at δ₀ ≈ 10⁻¹⁶. A system with Lyapunov exponent λ = 1 therefore loses all predictability after roughly 35 time units, no matter how good the solver is. Switch on the Butterfly twin in the simulator: it runs a second copy displaced by δ and measures λ for whatever system you load.

The solver

Regularized Bulirsch–Stoer

The equations blow up when two bodies meet: the 1/r³ term goes to infinity. An ordinary solver takes smaller and smaller steps there and either stalls or fails. This page integrates in a fictitious time s instead, chosen so that close encounters are stretched out:

{{EQ:logh}}

A leapfrog split of these equations reproduces any two-body Kepler orbit exactly, even straight through a collision (Mikkola & Tanikawa 1999; Preto & Tremaine 1999). Bulirsch–Stoer extrapolation then runs the leapfrog with 2, 4, 6, … substeps and extrapolates to zero step size. That gives roughly tenth-order accuracy plus an error estimate, which sets the step size automatically. The same algorithm runs here in JavaScript and in the repository's Python package. Everything below is checked by its test suite:

  • Burrau's Pythagorean problem (masses 3, 4, 5 released from rest) ends with mass 3 ejected at t ≈ 60 and masses 4 and 5 in a binary with eccentricity 0.99, as Szebehely and Peters found in 1967. Energy is conserved to 10⁻¹¹ through a close approach of 0.0006.
  • Agrees with SciPy's 8th-order DOP853 integrator to 10⁻⁵ after 15 time units of chaotic motion.
  • The figure-eight closes to 3 × 10⁻⁸ after one period. That limit comes from its 8-digit published starting values, not from the solver.
  • Lagrange triangles stay equilateral to 10⁻⁸, and Euler lines stay collinear, for unequal masses.
  • A head-on collision passes through cleanly, with energy error below 10⁻⁸.

Fate map

Who gets thrown out, and when?

Release three equal masses from rest. Bodies 1 and 2 start at (−½, 0) and (½, 0); body 3 starts at the point you pick. Almost every such system ends the same way: two bodies pair up and the third is flung away. Which one goes, and when, depends on the starting point in a way no formula can capture.

Each of the 96,000 pixels below is a full integration with the same solver, run until a body escapes or t = 200. Brightness shows how soon a body escapes; switch the view to see which one. Click any pixel to run that system in the simulator. It will play out exactly as computed here.

The map holds a built-in experiment. Its left and right halves are mirror images, so in exact arithmetic they would match pixel for pixel. Both halves were integrated independently, and their floating-point rounding differs in the 16th digit. In the smooth bands, where neighbouring starts share a fate, the halves agree on the escaping body for 99.9% of pixels. In the speckled regions they agree on only 64%. There, a difference of one part in 1016 decides which star is thrown out.
